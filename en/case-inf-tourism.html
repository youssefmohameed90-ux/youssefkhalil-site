Hajj and Umrah · Saudi Arabia

A service that became the brand itself

A mobility solution for elderly and disabled pilgrims, in a place where traditional advertising is restricted. The outcome: a single sponsor worth SAR 180M.

180MSAR sponsorship value
1one sponsor for the whole service
01

Background

During Hajj and Umrah, elderly and disabled pilgrims need a safe, comfortable way to move through very large crowds. This service was built specifically for them.

02

The challenge

The environment is tightly regulated and traditional advertising is restricted. No billboards, no campaigns in the form any brand is used to.

So the question was: how do you build a brand and bring in a sponsor where there is no advertising space?

03

The idea

Instead of looking for ad space, we made the service itself the platform: it is the brand, the sponsorship space and the visitor experience.

Visitors meet the brand while actually using the service, and the sponsor appears inside something that matters to people, not inside an ad.

04

What we did

  • Developed the business concept from the start.
  • Built the brand strategy.
  • Set the launch plan.
  • Designed the sponsorship model and took it to sponsors.
05

The result

One sponsor came in at SAR 180M.

My role

I developed the business and brand concept, and secured a SAR 180M sponsor.

The takeaway

When advertising is restricted, the service itself becomes the advertising.

The sponsor's name is confidential under the contract.

Real estate · Lead management

More sales with no extra ad spend

The project was receiving plenty of leads, and most opportunities stalled before a site visit. Within 90 days, on the same ad budget, contracts nearly tripled.

800M+EGP contracted sales value (CSV)
2.9×contracts, from 48 to 137
-65%ad cost per contract
-57%cost per site visit
01

The problem

Marketing believed the campaigns were doing their job, while the sales team believed the leads were not serious.

In the comparison period only 12% of leads reached a completed site visit, and fewer than 1% signed a contract.

02

What the numbers said

We reviewed the contact and opportunity records inside the CRM:

  • Actual contact with the customer did not exceed 50%.
  • Some leads were landing with an agent who was unavailable.
  • Follow-up stopped after a few attempts.
  • Many opportunities were marked weak before qualification was finished.
  • No regular follow-up to confirm visits or win back no-shows.
03

The decision

We kept the ad budget fixed and rebuilt the journey after sign-up.

04

What we did

  • A shared definition of a qualified lead based on budget, need and purchase timing.
  • Leads routed by agent availability, specialty and open workload.
  • An owner for every opportunity, with an alert and reassignment when first contact is late.
  • Different follow-up tracks by seriousness, combining calls and suitable messages.
  • Content answering recurring objections: location, delivery and payment.
  • Visits confirmed, location sent, and rescheduling made available.
  • A weekly meeting reviewing why opportunities are lost and how they move between stages.
05

The result

We compared two equal groups of leads, with equal spend and a 90-day follow-up period for each:

  • Actual contact rate rose from 50% to about 78%.
  • Opportunities stalled for lack of follow-up fell by more than half.
  • Completed site visits more than doubled.
  • Cost per site visit fell by about 57%.
  • Contracts from 48 to 137 units.
  • Lead-to-contract conversion rose from 0.96% to 2.74%.
  • Contracted Sales Value (CSV) exceeded EGP 800 million, nearly three times the comparison group.
  • Ad cost per contract fell by about 65%, with no increase in ad spend.

The case showed how much opportunity was being lost inside follow-up. When assessing profitability, the cost of setting up and running the system is counted separately.

My role

Rebuilding the customer journey with the sales team.

The takeaway

Before you buy more leads, find out where the ones you already have are going.

Figures are at contract signing and shown as ratios and approximate magnitudes to respect client confidentiality. What happens after signing, such as collections or cancellations, was outside my role.

Luxury perfumes · Saudi Arabia

A 12.8 ROAS, and the company was losing money

SAR 1.28M in sales from SAR 100K in ads, and no net profit to show for it. In two months net profit rose 180%.

+180%net profit
2.4profit per SAR of ad spend, up from 0.5
2months only
01

The problem

On paper everything looked excellent: SAR 100K in ads brought SAR 1.28M in sales. A ROAS of 12.8.

Yet the owner was not seeing money at the end of it.

02

What the numbers said

Once we calculated net profit after every cost, each riyal of advertising was returning only half a riyal of profit. A POAS of 0.5.

Sales were high, but a large share came from low-margin products, with shipping, returns and payment fees on top.

03

The decision

Stop measuring by sales and measure by profit. Campaigns get built around margin, not around the ROAS figure.

04

What we did

  • Reviewed every cost: product, shipping, returns and payment.
  • Calculated profitability for each product on its own.
  • Compared pricing against competitors.
  • Restructured campaigns by profit margin and acquisition cost.
  • Reduced returns by improving the customer experience.
05

The result

In two months ROAS came down from 12.8 to 8.2, profit per riyal of ads went from 0.5 to 2.4, and net profit rose 180%.

The ROAS number went down, and the company started making money.

My role

Consultant: I diagnosed the problem and led the restructuring of campaigns and pricing.

The takeaway

ROAS tells you how much you sold. POAS tells you how much you made.

Background

Real estate · Ramadan season · Cairo

We raised the budget in Ramadan while the whole market was cutting

A residential project in East Cairo. A week before Ramadan, cost per lead had reached EGP 420, and the debate was between stopping the campaigns or cutting the budget in half. We decided the opposite, and contracts doubled.

1B+EGP contracted sales value (CSV)
2×units contracted, from 99 to 201
-36%ad cost per contract
01

The problem

A week before Ramadan, cost per lead had reached EGP 420. The whole market cuts spend in Ramadan, and the question in the meeting was: do we stop the campaigns or cut them in half?

In the previous comparison period, 18% of leads reached a completed site visit, and 11% of visits ended in a contract.

02

What the numbers said

Before deciding, we went back to the data from the previous Ramadan:

  • The number of leads fell 22%.
  • Site-visit attendance rose 31%.
  • The close rate rose 19%.

The data pointed to an opportunity worth testing: fewer enquiries may come with more serious buyers, provided the way of reaching and following up with them changes.

03

The decision

We raised the budget by 30% instead of cutting it. The increase was rolled out gradually, and its continuation was tied to lead and visit quality.

04

What we did

  • Spend concentrated after iftar.
  • Campaigns split by the customer’s budget and need.
  • Payment plans and location shown clearly to reduce unsuitable enquiries.
  • A target for the first contact attempt: within 7 minutes during working hours.
  • Faster follow-up for ready buyers, and a different track for longer-term prospects.
  • Site visits at times that suit Ramadan, with confirmation, reminders and rescheduling.
  • Campaigns connected to the CRM and the on-site team.
  • A clear owner for every customer, and a logged reason for every opportunity as it advances or is lost.
05

The result

After 30 days:

  • Total leads fell 14%.
  • The number of qualified leads more than doubled.
  • Cost per qualified lead fell by about 40%.
  • Lead-to-visit conversion rose from 18% to about 30%.
  • The close rate from visit to contract rose from 11% to 15.5%.
  • Units contracted from 99 to 201.
  • Contracted Sales Value (CSV) exceeded EGP 1 billion, nearly double the comparison period.
  • Ad cost per contract fell by more than a third, despite the higher budget.

The deciding factor was better enquiry quality and a smoother path from lead to visit to close, not a larger number of leads.

My role

Managing the ad budget and leading the decision with sales and operations.

The takeaway

Fewer leads does not mean fewer sales. Ask the data before you cut the budget.

Manufacturing · Egypt

Fewer enquiries, four times the customers

A plastic packaging factory spent about EGP 90K in three months, received around 600 enquiries and won just two customers. Management believed sales could not close.

4×new customers, from 2 to 8
-75%cost to acquire a customer
2.4MEGP in first orders, up from 600K

The client’s name is withheld.

01

The problem

The factory makes packaging for cosmetics and detergents. Cost per enquiry was EGP 150, which looked like good performance in the report.

But out of 600 enquiries, only two became customers.

02

What we found

We went through the enquiries and calls one by one:

  • 420 enquiries were for quantities below the economic minimum for production.
  • 120 were for products or materials the factory does not offer.
  • Only 60 were a reasonable fit.
  • The ad said “packaging for all your needs at the best price”, and the page showed no minimum order.
  • Quotations took four days on average.

So the problem was the kind of demand marketing was generating, and how fast a good request got a clear quote.

03

What we did

  • Focused on cosmetics companies ordering 10,000 units or more, instead of addressing the whole market.
  • A new page showing packaging types, materials, the minimum order and lead time.
  • A new message: “Test a sample of your packaging before approving the production order.”
  • Search campaigns split by packaging type, excluding terms such as “by the piece” and “home use”.
  • The enquiry form now asks for quantity, use and timing.
  • An owner for every request, a quote within 24 hours, and follow-up after the sample is delivered.
04

The result

Over the following three months, on the same ad spend:

  • Enquiries from 600 to 240.
  • Qualified enquiries from 60 to 120.
  • Formal quotations from 18 to 48.
  • New customers from 2 to 8.
  • First-order value from EGP 600K to EGP 2.4M.
  • Ad cost per new customer from about EGP 45,000 to about EGP 11,000.

Cost per enquiry rose from EGP 150 to EGP 375, while the cost to acquire a customer fell 75%.

My role

Marketing manager at the agency running the account: I led the diagnosis, strategy and execution.

The takeaway

Fewer leads can be the best decision, if the ones left are the ones who buy.

01

The problem

One contract worth SAR 900K from 12 tenders. The ready explanation was that the company was “not known enough”.

02

What we found

We reviewed the opportunities with the tendering and operations teams:

  • 5 tenders required a track record or capabilities beyond what the company had.
  • 4 were outside the regions the company could serve efficiently.
  • Only 3 were a genuine fit.
  • The company profile was generic, with no documented response times or transition plan from the previous operator.
  • Proposals talked about headcount and equipment, while clients wanted service continuity, fewer breakdowns and faster response.

  • A focus on operating office buildings in two regions only.
  • Documented case studies of past projects, a sample monthly performance report, and a transition and operations plan.
  • A matrix linking every tender requirement to proof of the ability to deliver it.
  • Introductory meetings through official channels, with registration and qualification completed before opportunities were issued.

The whole programme and proposal preparation cost SAR 240K.

04

The result

In the following year:

  • Tenders entered from 12 to 5.
  • Contracts won from 1 to 2.
  • Win rate from 8.3% to 40%.
  • New contract value from SAR 900K to SAR 3.6M, on two-year terms.

Signed contract value is not collected revenue. Of these contracts, SAR 1.2M of work was delivered within the year studied.

My role
